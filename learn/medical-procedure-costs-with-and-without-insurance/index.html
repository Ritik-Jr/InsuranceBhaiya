Medical Procedure Costs With & Without Insurance: Urgent Care, MRI, CT Scans, and Surrogacy

What urgent care, an MRI, and a CT scan actually cost with and without insurance, plus a state-by-state look at surrogacy costs and insurance requirements in California and New York.

30-Second Key Takeaways
  • An urgent care visit with insurance typically costs a $25-$100 copay if you're in-network, versus $100-$300+ paying cash — but any tests, X-rays, or procedures added during the visit are usually billed separately from the base copay.
  • An MRI without insurance commonly ranges from $400 to $3,500, and the single biggest driver isn't the body part being scanned — it's whether you go to a freestanding imaging center (cheaper) or a hospital outpatient department (often 2-4x more for the identical scan).
  • A CT scan follows the same site-of-service logic as an MRI: self-pay cash prices run roughly $300-$3,200, and with insurance your share depends on whether your plan treats advanced imaging as its own coinsurance tier or bundles it into your regular deductible.
  • Surrogacy in California typically costs $150,000-$220,000+ even with insurance involved, because coverage generally applies only to specific pregnancy-related medical care — agency fees, legal costs, and surrogate compensation are paid separately and aren't something insurance touches.
  • New York surrogacy runs a comparable $100,000-$225,000+, but state law (the Child-Parent Security Act) legally mandates the intended parents provide the surrogate with health insurance through 12 months postpartum plus a $750,000 life insurance policy — insurance requirements that are simply optional industry practice in most other states.

These five searches span two very different worlds — everyday diagnostic care most people will need at some point, and a highly specialized, state-regulated process that only a small number of families go through. What they share is the same underlying question: what does insurance actually pay for, and what number are you left holding regardless? Below are real dollar ranges for each, plus the specific plan and state mechanics that explain why the final bill lands where it does. For the underlying deductible, copay, and coinsurance mechanics referenced throughout, see What Does Health Insurance Actually Cover?

How Much Is Urgent Care With Insurance?

With insurance, an in-network urgent care visit typically costs a flat copay of $25-$100 on most PPO and HMO plans, due at the time of the visit regardless of what the underlying provider charge actually is. If you're on a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) and haven't met your deductible yet, you'll instead owe the full negotiated rate — commonly $150-$300 for a standard visit — which counts toward your deductible rather than being reduced to a copay. Either way, this copay or negotiated rate covers only the base evaluation: a rapid strep or flu test, an X-ray, stitches, or any other add-on procedure performed during the same visit is billed as a separate line item, and each carries its own copay, coinsurance, or full charge depending on your specific plan design. If that visit leads to a referral for imaging, an MRI or CT scan is billed entirely separately again. For a condition where either urgent care or the ER could reasonably apply, the cost gap between the two settings is usually large enough on its own to make the decision.

How Much Does an MRI Cost Without Insurance?

Without insurance, an MRI typically costs $400 to $3,500 depending on the body part scanned, whether contrast dye is used (commonly adding $100-$300), and — more than either of those factors — where the scan is performed. A freestanding outpatient imaging center generally charges $400-$1,200 for a standard MRI, while the identical scan performed in a hospital's outpatient imaging department frequently runs $1,500-$3,000 or more, purely as a function of where it happens rather than any difference in the equipment or the radiologist reading the images. This is commonly called the site-of-service differential, and it's the single largest lever a self-pay patient has over the final price — asking your ordering physician for a referral to an independent imaging center rather than the hospital's own facility can cut the bill dramatically for the same test. A CT scan follows this exact same pricing logic, and so does a simpler X-ray or a pricier PET scan. See MRI vs. CT scan compared side by side for why your doctor orders one over the other in the first place.

How Much Is a CT Scan With Insurance?

CT scans follow essentially the same cost logic as an MRI: a self-pay cash price of roughly $300-$3,200 depending on body part, contrast, and — again — site of service, with a freestanding imaging center running well below a hospital outpatient department for the identical scan. With insurance, some plans apply a specific flat copay for advanced diagnostic imaging (commonly $100-$500) separate from your regular office-visit copay, while others simply apply standard coinsurance (typically 10-40%) once your deductible is met, with no procedure-specific flat fee at all. Because a CT scan is usually ordered urgently — following an ER or urgent care visit, a suspected fracture, or an acute abdominal complaint — patients have less practical ability to shop around by facility than with a routine MRI, which is part of why CT scan billing disputes are common even among insured patients.

How Much Does Surrogacy Cost With Insurance in California?

Even with insurance factored in, a full gestational surrogacy journey in California typically costs $150,000 to $220,000 or more. Insurance in this context covers only certain pregnancy-related medical expenses for the surrogate — prenatal visits, delivery, and pregnancy complications — while the largest cost categories (surrogate compensation, typically $50,000-$110,000; agency fees; and legal costs) are paid directly by the intended parents and are never something insurance touches. California requires independent legal counsel for both the intended parents and the gestational carrier before a pre-birth order can be secured, which pushes legal costs higher than in less-regulated states. A newer factor as of January 1, 2026: under California's SB 729, large-group health plans issued, amended, or renewed on or after that date must cover IVF and fertility treatment, which can meaningfully reduce the clinical portion of a California surrogacy budget for intended parents on an eligible large-group plan — though it does not touch the surrogate-compensation or legal-fee portions of the total. One Sacramento family's real numbers walk through exactly how this plays out when the new mandate meets the rest of a surrogacy budget — and it's worth understanding what health insurance does and doesn't cover for a surrogate pregnancy itself before assuming any plan, new mandate or not, touches more than the clinical slice of the total.

How Much Does Surrogacy Cost With Insurance in New York?

Total surrogacy costs in New York typically fall in a comparable $100,000-$225,000+ range — but New York is unusual in making certain insurance coverage a legal requirement rather than an optional best practice. Under the Child-Parent Security Act (CPSA), which legalized compensated gestational surrogacy in New York effective February 15, 2021, intended parents are legally required to provide the surrogate with comprehensive health insurance covering the entire process — from pre-transfer medications through pregnancy and for a full 12 months after the pregnancy ends — plus a separate life insurance policy of at least $750,000 that also stays in effect for those same 12 months. New York is also the only state that requires surrogacy matching programs and agencies working with New York surrogates to be licensed by the state Department of Health, which adds a layer of cost and structure not present in most other states' surrogacy markets.

Why California and New York Price and Regulate Surrogacy So Differently

Both states land in a similar total-cost range, but the insurance and legal mechanics behind that number differ in an important way. California's higher cost is driven mainly by market forces — high surrogate compensation, high demand, and a competitive fertility-clinic market — layered on top of a legal requirement for independent counsel on both sides. New York's cost is shaped more directly by statute: the CPSA doesn't just permit compensated surrogacy, it specifies exactly what insurance a surrogate must receive and for how long, which is why insurance-related costs are called out as their own explicit line item in New York surrogacy budgets in a way they generally aren't elsewhere. For intended parents comparing the two, the practical takeaway is the same regardless of state: get the surrogate's existing health insurance reviewed for a surrogacy exclusion before assuming her policy will apply, since most standard individual and employer plans exclude gestational-carrier pregnancies outright, often making a supplemental or dedicated surrogacy insurance policy — separate from ordinary health insurance — a necessary purchase in either state.

What About X-Rays, PET Scans, and the ER?

The imaging cluster extends beyond MRI and CT. An X-ray is the cheapest and most common diagnostic image, typically $100-$400 at an independent facility, and it's frequently the first step before a doctor even considers ordering an MRI. At the other end of the cost spectrum, a PET scan — most often used for cancer staging and monitoring — runs $1,000-$6,000 or more, priced higher than MRI or CT because of the radioactive tracer it requires, and it almost always needs prior authorization before your insurer will pay. And when a condition genuinely could go either way between urgent care and the emergency room, running the cost comparison side by side makes clear just how much the site-of-service gap can matter — an ER copay alone commonly exceeds the entire cost of an urgent care visit.

What About Ultrasounds, Colonoscopies, and Lab Work?

Not every common medical cost question involves an MRI-scale bill. An ultrasound is generally the least expensive imaging option after an X-ray, typically $200-$1,000 without insurance depending on the body area, and a routine pregnancy ultrasound is often bundled into global maternity billing rather than charged separately. A colonoscopy sits in an unusual category of its own: an average-risk screening colonoscopy should cost $0 out of pocket by federal rule, including when a polyp is found and removed, though hospitals sometimes bill it incorrectly as diagnostic anyway — a mistake worth knowing how to appeal. The same screening-versus-diagnostic distinction applies to routine blood work and lab panels, where a preventive annual panel is typically free but a test added because of a symptom mentioned during the same visit is billed separately.

One more cost trap worth knowing about applies across almost every procedure in this guide: confirming a hospital or imaging center is in-network doesn't guarantee every provider involved in your care is too. One patient's real experience with an out-of-network radiologist bill after an in-network CT scan shows exactly how this happens — and what federal protections exist to fix it.

Frequently Asked Questions & Related Resources

Common search queries answered with direct links to companion calculators and guides.

Does insurance cover the full cost of an urgent care visit?
Usually not entirely — most plans apply a flat copay ($25-$100 is typical) to the visit itself if you haven't met your deductible for a copay-based plan, but any labs, X-rays, or procedures performed during that same visit are typically billed and reimbursed separately, which is the most common source of a bill that's higher than expected.
Why is an MRI so much cheaper at a freestanding imaging center than at a hospital?
Hospitals generally negotiate higher reimbursement rates with insurers than independent imaging centers do for the identical scan, partly to cover overhead the hospital carries that a standalone facility doesn't — this is often called the site-of-service differential, and it can make the same MRI cost two to four times more at a hospital outpatient department.
Does health insurance ever directly cover the cost of surrogacy itself?
No — standard health insurance covers specific pregnancy-related medical care (prenatal visits, delivery, complications) for the person who is pregnant, but it does not cover surrogate compensation, agency fees, legal services, or the IVF and embryo transfer costs that make up the bulk of a surrogacy journey's total price.
