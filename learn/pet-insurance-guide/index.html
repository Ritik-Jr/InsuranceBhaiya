Pet Insurance Explained: What It Covers, What It Costs & What Gets Excluded

Pet insurance covers accidents and illness, not everything. See real costs, reimbursement math, waiting periods and exclusions like desexing, dental and cremation.

IB
Insurance Bhaiya Research Desk Peer-Reviewed
Actuarial Research Fellow • Updated October 2026 • 6 min read
100% Unbiased • No Kickbacks
30-Second Key Takeaways
  • Pet insurance is reimbursement insurance: you pay the vet first, then the insurer repays a percentage (commonly 70%, 80% or 90%) after your deductible.
  • Standard accident-and-illness plans usually exclude routine care such as spay/neuter (desexing), vaccines, cleanings and cremation unless you add a wellness plan or end-of-life benefit.
  • Pre-existing conditions and waiting periods are the two biggest reasons claims are denied, so enrol while your pet is young and healthy.
  • Dental illness is covered by some comprehensive plans, but routine dental cleanings almost never are.
  • Insurance beats saving when a single surprise bill (typically $3,000 to $8,000 for surgery) would force debt or a euthanasia decision you do not want to make.

A single emergency vet visit now routinely costs $1,500 to $5,000, and complex surgery can pass $8,000. Pet insurance exists to turn that unpredictable spike into a predictable monthly premium. But it is also one of the most misunderstood policies in personal insurance: owners assume it works like human health insurance, then discover that desexing, dental cleaning, cremation and anything diagnosed before enrolment are not paid. If you are still deciding whether it is worth buying at all, start with our straight answer on is pet insurance worth it, then use this guide to read any policy with open eyes.

How Pet Insurance Actually Works

Almost all pet policies are reimbursement plans. You visit any licensed vet, pay the full invoice, submit the claim, and the insurer sends back a percentage of the eligible amount. Three levers decide how much you recover: the annual or per-condition deductible, the reimbursement rate (70%, 80% or 90%), and the annual or lifetime benefit limit.

Reimbursement Math in One Example

Your dog swallows a sock and needs a $4,000 obstruction surgery. With a $500 deductible and 80% reimbursement: ($4,000 - $500) x 80% = $2,800 paid by the insurer. You keep $1,200 of the bill. On a plan with a $5,000 annual limit, $2,200 of limit remains for the rest of the policy year. Model your own numbers with our Deductible Breakeven Calculator.

What Pet Insurance Typically Covers

  • Accidents: broken bones, bite wounds, ingested foreign objects, toxin exposure, torn ligaments from a fall.
  • Illnesses: infections, cancer, diabetes, allergies, gastrointestinal disease, ear and skin conditions (on accident-and-illness plans).
  • Diagnostics and hospital care: X-rays, ultrasound, bloodwork, MRI, overnight stays and prescription medication tied to a covered condition.
  • Hereditary and chronic conditions: many comprehensive plans cover hip dysplasia or diabetes if the condition was not present before enrolment and waiting periods were served.
  • Emergency and specialty care: most plans let you use any licensed vet or specialty hospital.

What Pet Insurance Usually Excludes

The exclusion section of a pet policy is where owners get surprised. The four most common gaps are below, each with its own deep dive.

  1. Pre-existing conditions: anything with signs, symptoms or a diagnosis before coverage starts or during the waiting period. Read the full rules in does pet insurance cover pre-existing conditions.
  2. Spay, neuter and desexing: an elective, preventive procedure that standard plans do not pay. See does pet insurance cover desexing for when a wellness add-on makes sense.
  3. Routine dental cleaning: coverage is limited to accidental tooth injuries and, on some plans, dental disease. Details are in does pet insurance cover dental.
  4. Cremation, burial and disposal: treated as post-death expenses, not medical care. Learn the exceptions in does pet insurance cover cremation.

Waiting Periods and Pre-Existing Conditions

Even after you buy a policy, coverage does not start on day one for everything. A pet insurance waiting period commonly runs about 14 days for illness, a few days for accidents, and up to 6 months or more for orthopedic conditions like cruciate ligament tears. Any problem that appears during that window can become a pre-existing condition, which is excluded for life by many insurers.

The Costliest Mistake: Waiting Until Something Goes Wrong

Insurers can review your pet's full veterinary records before approving a large claim. If a vet note from before enrolment mentions limping, itching, vomiting or ear problems, the related claim can be denied. Enrol while your pet is young and healthy, and ask the insurer in writing how it treats curable conditions.

Routine Care: Wellness Plans, Desexing and Dental

A pet wellness plan is not insurance in the strict sense. It is a prepaid allowance with fixed per-service benefits for vaccines, exams and sometimes spay/neuter or cleaning. Because you are paying premium to get your own money back, compare the annual add-on cost to the benefits you will really use. For a puppy needing vaccines plus a spay or neuter, it can break even; for a healthy adult cat, often not.

End-of-Life Costs

Many plans reimburse euthanasia when it is part of treating a covered illness, but cremation or burial is usually your cost, commonly around $50 to $150 for communal cremation and $150 to $500 or more for a private one depending on size. A small number of insurers offer a limited end-of-life benefit or rider. Check the policy language before assuming, and see does pet insurance cover cremation.

How to Choose a Pet Insurance Policy

  1. Pick the highest annual limit you can afford; a $5,000 cap can be exhausted by one surgery.
  2. Choose a deductible you can pay from savings without stress, and test the trade-off in our Deductible Breakeven Calculator.
  3. Prefer 80% to 90% reimbursement if the monthly premium fits your budget.
  4. Read the exclusions list, waiting periods and bilateral-condition rules before paying.
  5. Check whether dental illness, hereditary conditions, prescription food and alternative therapies are included.
  6. Run your current protection through our 8-Point Policy Check and review how to read an insurance policy.

Frequently Asked Questions & Related Resources

Common search queries answered with direct links to companion calculators and guides.

What does pet insurance not cover?
Most plans exclude pre-existing conditions, routine and preventive care (vaccines, spay/neuter, cleanings), cosmetic procedures, breeding and pregnancy costs, and cremation or burial. See does pet insurance cover pre-existing conditions and does pet insurance cover cremation.
Does pet insurance cover dental cleaning?
Routine cleaning is generally not covered on standard plans. Accidental tooth injuries usually are, and some comprehensive plans cover dental disease. Read does pet insurance cover dental before you buy.
Does pet insurance cover spay or neuter surgery?
Only through wellness add-ons or certain preventive packages. Standard accident-and-illness plans treat it as elective. See does pet insurance cover desexing.
How long is the waiting period for pet insurance?
Typical waiting periods are a few days for accidents, about 14 days for illness and up to 6 months for orthopedic conditions, though they vary. Learn the definition in our glossary entry on the pet insurance waiting period.
Is pet insurance cheaper if I buy when my pet is a puppy or kitten?
Yes. Premiums rise with age and conditions diagnosed early become pre-existing exclusions later. Compare tiers in accident-only vs. comprehensive pet insurance and run the numbers on our Coverage Calculator.
