Car Insurance Basics: Registration Requirements, Cost Factors, and How Claims Work

Car insurance basics covering state registration requirements, what actually drives cost, and how claims, rentals, and coverage gaps really work.

IB
Insurance Bhaiya Research Desk Peer-Reviewed
Actuarial Research Fellow • Updated September 2026 • 3 min read
100% Unbiased • No Kickbacks
30-Second Key Takeaways
  • Every state ties vehicle registration to proof of insurance, but the specific minimums vary sharply — New York and Florida require no-fault personal injury protection on top of liability, while Indiana requires only liability coverage.
  • California's minimum liability limits doubled on January 1, 2025 (from 15/30/5 to 30/60/15) under Senate Bill 1107 — the first increase in nearly 60 years, and a major driver of recent premium increases alongside the state's dense traffic and high litigation costs.
  • Florida is one of the only states that doesn't require bodily injury liability insurance for most drivers, relying instead on no-fault PIP — an unusual structure worth understanding before assuming Florida's 'low' minimums mean weak coverage overall.
  • How long an insurer pays for a rental car depends entirely on which coverage is paying: your own rental reimbursement add-on has a fixed daily and total cap, while a claim against an at-fault driver's insurer typically covers a 'reasonable period' tied to actual repair time.
  • Standard car insurance covers accident and covered-peril damage, not mechanical breakdown or normal wear — a distinction that surprises many drivers expecting a repair claim to be covered when the actual cause was a worn-out part.

Car insurance searches cluster around three practical questions: do I legally need this to register my car, and how much (which varies significantly by state), why does this cost so much (especially in high-cost states like California), and how do claims, rentals, and coverage gaps actually work once something happens. This guide covers all three, with a link to the full answer on each specific question.

A standard personal auto policy covers accident and named-peril damage, not everything you might assume. State Minimums Are a Legal Floor, Not a Safety Recommendation

Every state's minimum liability requirement is the least you're legally allowed to carry, not the amount that would actually protect your finances after a serious accident — this is especially true in states like Florida and Indiana, which don't require bodily injury liability or PIP at meaningfully protective levels.

Frequently Asked Questions & Related Resources

Common search queries answered with direct links to companion calculators and guides.

Which state has the highest minimum car insurance requirements?
Requirements vary by category — New York's $50,000 no-fault PIP requirement is among the highest for that specific coverage, while other states set higher bodily injury liability minimums. There's no single state with the highest minimum across every coverage type; it depends on which coverage you're comparing.
Do I need car insurance if I don't drive my car regularly?
In most states, yes — insurance is generally tied to active vehicle registration, not how often you drive, so a registered vehicle typically needs continuous coverage. Some states allow you to avoid this by formally declaring a vehicle non-operational and surrendering the plates instead.

Can an insurance company drop you or cancel your policy after you file a claim?

In most jurisdictions, an insurer cannot immediately cancel your policy mid-term solely because you filed a single legitimate claim, unless there was proven fraud, material misrepresentation, or non-payment.
