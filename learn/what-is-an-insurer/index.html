What Is an Insurer? Carriers, Solvency, and Financial Strength Ratings

Discover what an insurance company (carrier) is, how stock vs mutual insurers differ, how carriers maintain capital reserves, and why AM Best financial ratings matter.

IB
Marcus Vance
Actuarial Research Fellow • Fact-Checked

Key Editorial Takeaways

  • Review your current declarations page to confirm exact deductibles, sub-limits, and named insured designations.
  • Policy exclusions supersede marketing summaries—always verify specific peril exceptions in Section I and II.
  • Adjust coverage whenever life circumstances change (e.g., mortgage refinancing, marriage, or acquiring high-value assets).

An insurer—frequently referred to as an insurance carrier or underwriting company—is the licensed financial institution that assumes contractual liability for covered losses, collects premiums, and holds legal responsibility for settling claims.

Stock Companies vs. Mutual Companies

Not all insurers are organized under the same corporate structure. The two dominant structures have distinctly different fiduciary priorities:

FeatureStock Insurance CompanyMutual Insurance Company
OwnershipPublic shareholders holding equity stockThe policyholders themselves
Corporate PriorityMaximizing shareholder returns and stock dividendsServing policyholder longevity and lowering net cost
Surplus ProfitsPaid out as quarterly cash dividends to Wall Street investorsDistributed back to policyholders as policy dividends or reduced premiums
Prominent ExamplesTravelers, Progressive, AllstateNorthwestern Mutual, State Farm, Guardian Life
Checking Carrier Solvency: Financial Ratings

Never buy a policy solely based on low premiums without checking the carrier's financial solvency. Credit rating agencies evaluate an insurer's ability to pay claims during massive economic crises. Look for an 'A' rating or higher from AM Best Rating Services or Fitch Ratings.

What Happens If an Insurer Goes Bankrupt?

If an admitted carrier becomes insolvent, state statutory safety nets known as State Guaranty Associations step in. These associations transfer active policies to solvent carriers or pay claims up to statutory state caps (typically $300,000 to $500,000 per policyholder), coordinated via the National Organization of Life & Health Insurance Guaranty Associations (NOLHGA).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an admitted vs non-admitted carrier?
An admitted carrier is fully licensed by your state's insurance department and backed by the state guaranty fund. A non-admitted carrier (surplus lines) writes high-risk or unusual policies without state guaranty fund backing.
Financial ratings change periodically. Verify your carrier's current balance sheet before committing to multi-decade contracts.
