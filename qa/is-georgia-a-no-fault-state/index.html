Is Georgia a no-fault state?

IB
Insurance Bhaiya Auto Claims Group Verified Answer
Reviewed by Licensed Auto Claims Adjuster & Georgia Tort Specialist • Updated September 2026 • 6 min read
Zero Commission Bias
Quick Answer / Executive Summary

No. Georgia is an at-fault (tort) state, not a no-fault state. The driver who causes a crash is responsible for the other people's injuries and property damage through their liability insurance. Georgia requires 25/50/25 liability. Uninsured motorist coverage is automatically part of every policy unless you reject it in writing. Georgia's 50% comparative fault rule means you recover nothing if you're 50% or more to blame.

Key Takeaways at a Glance
  • Georgia is an at-fault state. Claims for injuries and car damage go against the at-fault driver and their insurer. See how this differs from no-fault in the no-fault vs at-fault states guide.
  • The minimum liability is 25/50/25: $25,000 per person, $50,000 per crash for bodily injury, and $25,000 property damage.
  • Georgia doesn't require PIP. Medical payments (MedPay) coverage is optional but useful while fault is being decided. See PIP vs MedPay.
  • Uninsured motorist coverage is built into Georgia policies unless you reject it in writing, and 'added-on' UM is the default. See is uninsured motorist coverage required in Georgia.
  • Georgia uses modified comparative negligence with a 50% bar, and you generally have two years to file an injury lawsuit.

Is Georgia a No-Fault State or an At-Fault State?

Georgia is firmly an at-fault state. It has never used the PIP-first model found in New York or Florida. After a Georgia crash:

• The at-fault driver's liability insurance pays your medical bills, lost income, pain and suffering and vehicle damage, up to their limits.
• You can file a third-party claim with that insurer, use your own coverage first (collision, MedPay, UM) and let your insurer recover the money through subrogation, or file a lawsuit.
• There's no injury threshold to sue. Minor injuries can still support a pain-and-suffering claim, which isn't true in no-fault states with a tort threshold.

Like Georgia, Texas, Colorado and most Southern states are at-fault states. See is Texas a no-fault state and is Colorado a no-fault state.
Why People Think Georgia Is No-Fault

Florida, right next door, is a no-fault state with mandatory PIP, and many Georgia drivers commute across the line or moved from there. The rules change at the state line. In Florida your PIP pays first, and in Georgia the at-fault driver pays. See are you required to have car insurance in Florida.

Georgia Car Insurance Requirements

Required liability (25/50/25):
• $25,000 bodily injury per person
• $50,000 bodily injury per crash
• $25,000 property damage

Automatically included unless rejected in writing:
• Uninsured motorist (UM) at least equal to the minimum limits, under O.C.G.A. §33-7-11.

Optional but worth considering:
• MedPay, which pays medical bills regardless of fault
• Collision and comprehensive. See comprehensive vs collision.
• Rental reimbursement. See how long will insurance pay for a rental car after an accident.

Driving uninsured in Georgia can lead to suspended registration and personal liability for everything. See is it against the law to drive without insurance.
25/50/25 Rarely Covers a Serious Crash

Atlanta traffic, trucks and rising medical costs mean one serious crash can use up $25,000 in a single ambulance ride and surgery. Model safer limits with the car insurance calculator and read how to know if you are underinsured.

Georgia's 50% Comparative Fault Rule

Georgia uses modified comparative negligence with a 50% bar (Bloomberg Law chart):

• 10% at fault: you recover 90% of your damages.
• 49% at fault: you recover 51%.
• 50% or more at fault: you recover nothing from the other driver.

That's one percentage point stricter than Texas, which bars recovery at 51%. Because a 50/50 split wipes out your claim, how fault is documented matters a great deal. Photograph the scene, get witness details, request the police report, and think twice before a recorded statement to the other insurer. If the insurer drags its feet, see can I sue my insurance company for taking too long.

What If the Driver Who Hit You Is Uninsured?

Then Georgia's UM rules are what save you. Because UM is included unless you rejected it in writing, and 'added-on' is the default, your UM coverage can pay in addition to whatever limited liability the other driver carries. That's a big advantage over 'reduced-by' coverage. The full breakdown is in is uninsured motorist coverage required in Georgia, and you can follow a real-life example in our Atlanta uninsured driver scenario.

Real-Life Case Incident & Precedent
Precedent: Georgia at-fault tort liability; O.C.G.A. §33-7-11 added-on uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage

Illustrative Case: Keisha's Rear-End Crash on I-285

Scenario: Keisha was stopped in traffic on I-285 when a driver rear-ended her at 35 mph. She had whiplash and a concussion, $11,500 in medical bills, $2,400 in lost wages and $6,800 in car damage. The other driver carried 25/50/25 liability. Keisha had $5,000 of MedPay and added-on UM of 50/100.

Resolution & Judicial Outcome: Rear-end fault was clear, so the other driver's insurer accepted 100% liability. Keisha's MedPay paid $5,000 of her early bills within weeks. The other driver's $25,000 bodily injury limit went toward her remaining medical bills, wages and pain and suffering, and his $25,000 property damage limit covered her car. When her total injury claim was valued at $38,000, her added-on UM paid the $13,000 above his limit.

What You Should Do: Step-by-Step Action Plan

1 Check your policy: confirm UM is present and whether it's 'added-on' or 'reduced-by'.
2 Add MedPay so medical bills get paid while fault is decided.
3 Raise liability above 25/50/25 if you have savings, home equity or future earnings to protect.
4 After a crash, report it to police, gather evidence, and file with the at-fault insurer and your own.
5 Keep the two-year lawsuit deadline in mind if the claim stalls.

Critical Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming Georgia works like Florida's no-fault system.
  • Signing a UM rejection form without understanding it.
  • Accepting a 50/50 fault split without challenging it. In Georgia, that split wipes out your claim.
  • Settling before you know the full extent of your injuries. Read can you sue someone after settling with their insurance.

Can an insurance company drop you or cancel your policy after you file a claim?

In most jurisdictions, an insurer cannot immediately cancel your policy mid-term solely because you filed a single legitimate claim, unless there was proven fraud, material misrepresentation, or non-payment. However, they are legally permitted to non-renew your policy when the term expires (typically every 6 or 12 months) by providing advance written notice.

What happens legally and financially if you get into a car crash without insurance?

Driving without insurance and causing an accident results in catastrophic personal financial liability and severe legal penalties. You are personally liable for 100% of all medical bills, property damages, and legal judgments out of pocket. Legally, authorities can suspend your driver's license, impound your vehicle, impose thousands in fines, and mandate SR-22 high-risk insurance for years.

Why did my car insurance premium increase even though I had no accidents or tickets?

Car insurance rates can increase substantially without any tickets or accidents due to macroeconomic rate revisions, rising vehicle repair costs, increased frequency of catastrophic weather losses in your zip code, insurance credit score changes, and widespread insurance industry underwriting adjustments across your state.

Does your personal auto insurance cover rental cars, or do you need rental counter coverage?

In the United States and Canada, your personal auto insurance coverage (comprehensive, collision, and liability) typically transfers directly to a rental car driven for personal pleasure, up to your existing policy limits and deductibles. However, personal policies almost never cover 'loss of use' administrative fees charged by rental companies, and do not provide coverage internationally.
