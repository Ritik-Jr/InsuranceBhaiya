Who is exempt from workers' compensation insurance in California?

IB
Insurance Bhaiya Legal Group Verified Answer
Reviewed by Licensed Commercial Lines Broker & Actuarial Auditor • Updated October 2026 • 7 min read
Zero Commission Bias
Quick Answer / Executive Summary

Almost no California employer is exempt: if you employ one or more people, you must carry workers' compensation. The exemptions are narrow and mostly concern owners. A sole proprietor with no employees need not cover themselves, a sole-shareholder officer is generally excluded unless they elect cover, and certain officers, directors, partners and members may waive coverage by signed waiver. Some worker categories, such as genuine independent contractors, are also outside the system.

Key Takeaways at a Glance
  • Hiring even one part-time employee triggers the requirement to secure workers' compensation in California.
  • Owner exemptions are about who counts as an employee: sole proprietors, sole-shareholder officers, and certain officers, partners and managing members who meet ownership rules and sign a written waiver.
  • Waivers must be signed under penalty of perjury and accepted by the insurer. Ownership thresholds have changed over the years, so confirm the current rule with your insurer or the state.
  • Going uninsured is expensive: stop orders, penalties and personal liability for injury costs. Read the wider picture in the business insurance coverage and requirements guide and the liability gaps in does business insurance cover lawsuits.

The Baseline Rule: One Employee Means Coverage

California requires every employer with at least one employee, full-time or part-time, to secure workers' compensation (Labor Code §3700). The law defines 'employee' broadly in Labor Code §3351, including minors and workers paid in cash. Exemptions therefore work by excluding a person from the definition of employee, not by exempting the business.

The insurance pays employee medical treatment, temporary and permanent disability benefits and death benefits regardless of fault. In exchange, workers' compensation is generally the employee's exclusive remedy against the employer for covered injuries, which is why it matters for legal risk. Compare this with third-party lawsuit protection in does business insurance cover lawsuits.
Cash Paid Does Not Make Someone a Contractor

Misclassifying employees as independent contractors is a leading cause of uninsured workers' comp exposure. California applies a strict test; labels in a contract are not decisive.

Who Can Be Exempt or Excluded

1. Sole proprietors with no employees. You are not an employee of your own business, so you need not cover yourself. You may elect to be included. Note that licensed contractors face stricter state licensing rules, so check with the Contractors State License Board.

2. Sole-shareholder officers and directors. The sole shareholder of a private corporation is generally excluded automatically unless they elect coverage.

3. Other officers and directors. They may be excluded if they meet the ownership threshold and sign a written waiver that the insurer endorses on the policy. Current guidance and forms point to ownership of at least 10% of the corporation's stock; older rules used 15%, so confirm the threshold in force when you apply.

4. General partners and managing members of an LLC. They can waive coverage by written waiver; ordinary working members are treated as employees if paid.

5. Genuine independent contractors, volunteers for certain organizations and some household workers. A domestic worker who works fewer than 52 hours, or earns under $100, in the 90 days before an injury is excluded.

Every exclusion needs paperwork. A waiver gives up the owner's own benefits, so weigh that against your health and disability cover. For wider exemption rules outside California, see our workers' compensation exemptions hub.

What Happens If You Skip Coverage You Needed

An employer who fails to secure coverage risks stop orders that halt operations, substantial penalties (potentially thousands of dollars per employee), and direct liability for the injured worker's medical and wage-loss benefits. The injured employee may also pursue civil claims, and in many cases state funds advance benefits and then pursue the employer for reimbursement.

Costs of compliance are modest by comparison: premiums follow payroll and job class. Before you decide not to buy, run through how much business insurance do I need and confirm how the policy will interact with your other coverages. If you later need to drop a policy, remember that going without replacement is itself a violation; see can you cancel business insurance at any time.
Real-Life Case Incident & Precedent
Precedent: California Labor Code §3351 (definition of employee), §3352 (exclusions) and §3700 (duty to secure payment of compensation)

Case Study: Tomas's Cabinet Shop & the Part-Time Helper

Scenario: Tomas owns 100% of a California S-corporation that builds custom cabinets. As sole shareholder and officer he was automatically excluded from his own coverage, so he bought no policy. Months later he hired a part-time helper at about $900 per month. The helper's hand was injured by a table saw, producing $64,000 in medical bills and lost wages.

Resolution & Judicial Outcome: Because Tomas had an employee, he was required to carry coverage even though he himself was excluded. With no policy, the state's uninsured-employer process paid benefits and pursued Tomas for reimbursement plus penalties, and the helper retained the right to sue him. A workers' compensation policy for the helper's wages would have cost a small fraction of the loss.

What You Should Do: Step-by-Step Action Plan

1 Step 1: Count everyone who performs services for you, including part-time and cash-paid workers, and classify each correctly.
2 Step 2: Identify your entity type (sole proprietorship, corporation, partnership or LLC) and who the owners and officers are.
3 Step 3: If you want an owner excluded, obtain the insurer's waiver form, check the ownership threshold and sign it under penalty of perjury.
4 Step 4: Buy a policy for all employees and confirm the insurer has endorsed any waivers on the policy.
5 Step 5: Re-check whenever you hire; see how much business insurance do I need for sizing the rest of your insurance stack.

Critical Mistakes to Avoid

  • Believing a sole shareholder's exclusion removes the duty to cover other employees.
  • Paying workers in cash or labelling them contractors to avoid premium without meeting the contractor test.
  • Assuming a waiver is effective before the insurer accepts and endorses it.
  • Relying on a blog's ownership percentage instead of checking the current rule with your insurer or the state.

Can I refuse a recorded statement to insurance company?

Yes, you have an absolute legal right to refuse a recorded statement to the OTHER driver's insurance company (third-party insurer). You are under no legal obligation to speak with them or be recorded. However, you generally CANNOT refuse a statement to your OWN insurance company (first-party insurer), because your policy contract contains a mandatory 'Duty to Cooperate' clause. Refusing to cooperate with your own insurer can lead to claim denial or policy cancellation.

Can you sue someone after settling with their insurance?

No, in virtually all circumstances, you cannot sue someone after settling with their insurance company. When you accept an insurance settlement check, the insurance company requires you to sign a legally binding document called a 'Release of All Claims'. This contract permanently extinguishes your right to pursue the at-fault party or their insurer for any additional damages, medical bills, or pain and suffering arising from that accident, even if new injuries appear later.

Can you sue your insurance company?

Yes, you can legally sue your insurance company if they breach the insurance contract, unreasonably deny a valid claim, undervalue property damage, or act in 'bad faith'. Lawsuits against insurers generally fall under two legal doctrines: Breach of Contract (for failing to pay benefits owed under the written policy terms) and Insurance Bad Faith (for egregious, deceptive, or unfair claim settlement practices, which can entitle you to punitive damages and attorney fees).

Can I sue my insurance company for taking too long?

Yes, you can sue your insurance company for taking too long to resolve or pay your claim. Most states have enacted 'Prompt Payment of Claims' statutes that require insurance carriers to acknowledge, investigate, and approve or deny claims within strict deadlines (typically 15 to 30 days). When an insurer uses unreasonable delays to wear you down or force a cheap settlement, their conduct constitutes insurance bad faith, entitling you to file a lawsuit for statutory interest, damages, and attorney fees.
