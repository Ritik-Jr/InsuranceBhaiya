Is title insurance worth it?

IB
Insurance Bhaiya Home Group Verified Answer
Reviewed by Licensed Claims Adjuster & Actuarial Auditor • Updated October 2026 • 5 min read
Zero Commission Bias
Quick Answer / Executive Summary

For most buyers, an owner's title policy is worth it because it is a one-time cost, usually well under 1% of the purchase price, that protects the largest asset you will own against defects from before you bought. A lender's policy is required if you have a mortgage, but it only protects the lender. Title claims are uncommon, so the value lies in covering a low-probability, high-cost loss such as a forged deed or an old unpaid lien.

Key Takeaways at a Glance
  • An owner's policy protects your equity for as long as you or your heirs own the home; the lender's policy does not.
  • The premium is one-time, and combined policies typically run about 0.5% to 1% of the price.
  • The value is protection against a rare but expensive loss, not frequent claims.
  • It matters most for older homes, foreclosures, estate sales, for-sale-by-owner deals and purchases without a survey.
  • Cash buyers can skip it, but the saving is small against the potential loss.

The Math: Small Premium, Large Potential Loss

On a $400,000 home, combined lender's and owner's policies typically cost about $2,000 to $4,000, with industry data putting the median near 0.67% (about $2,680). That is the price of insuring an asset worth $400,000. A single title dispute can cost far more in legal fees alone, before you count any loss of the property or the equity in it.

Most title problems never occur, so the policy works like other insurance: you pay a small, certain sum to avoid a large, uncertain one. For the pricing details, see how much does title insurance cost and who pays.

When It Is Most Worth It

The case is strongest when the paperwork is likely to be messy: older homes with many past owners, foreclosures, estate sales, for-sale-by-owner purchases and sales between relatives. It also matters if you are putting down a large deposit or have little cash to fight a dispute.

What it protects is spelled out in what does title insurance cover. Remember that a title search is not a guarantee; it reduces risk but cannot catch forgery or hidden heirs.

When It May Matter Less

A cash buyer with strong legal advice, a clean recent chain of title and a high tolerance for risk might reasonably decline. Even then, weigh the premium against the size of your investment and how long you plan to stay. If you do borrow, the lender's policy is mandatory regardless, as covered in do I need owner's title insurance if my lender requires a policy.

Real-Life Case Incident & Precedent
Precedent: ALTA standard owner's policy forms: covered risks include liens, forgery and recording errors existing at the policy date; exceptions and exclusions apply

Illustrative Case: Dana and the Unreleased Mortgage

Scenario: Dana buys a 1960s house for $350,000 and takes out both policies at closing. Two years later a bank claims that a mortgage from a former owner, which was paid off decades ago, was never formally released and still shows as a lien.

Resolution & Judicial Outcome: Because the lien existed before Dana's policy date and was not listed as an exception, her title insurer pays to clear the record and covers the legal costs. Without an owner's policy she would have had to pay for the cleanup herself. The scenario is illustrative; outcomes depend on the policy terms.

What You Should Do: Step-by-Step Action Plan

1 Ask the title company for a quote for the lender's policy alone and for both policies together.
2 Check the commitment's exceptions to see what the policy will not cover.
3 Consider the age of the home, how it is being sold and whether you have a survey.
4 Negotiate who pays for the owner's policy during contract talks.
5 Compare your cash cushion with the legal costs of defending a title dispute.

Critical Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming the lender's policy protects you.
  • Declining an owner's policy only to save a modest premium on a six-figure asset.
  • Not reading the exceptions section of the title commitment.
  • Believing a clean title search means a policy is unnecessary.
