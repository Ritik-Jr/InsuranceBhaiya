Do dirt bikes need insurance, and what states require dirt bike insurance?

IB
Insurance Bhaiya Auto Claims Group Verified Answer
Reviewed by Licensed Property & Casualty Underwriter • Updated September 2026 • 7 min read
Zero Commission Bias
Quick Answer / Executive Summary

Usually not on private land, but it depends on the state and where you ride. Most states don't require insurance on a dirt bike ridden only off-road on property you own. Some require liability coverage once you ride on public land or on property that isn't yours, or once the bike is registered. Pennsylvania and New York are among them. A dirt bike plated for street use (a dual-sport) needs regular motorcycle insurance in nearly every state.

Key Takeaways at a Glance
  • Legal requirements for dirt bike insurance are set by where you ride (private land, public trails, OHV parks or public roads), not by the bike itself.
  • Pennsylvania requires liability insurance for registered ATVs ridden on land you don't own or lease. New York requires ATV registration, its definition covers vehicles on two or more wheels, and liability coverage is generally required off the owner's property.
  • Many western states (such as Colorado) require OHV registration or trail permits for public land, but not insurance.
  • Even where it isn't required, a dirt bike crash that injures a friend or damages property can bring a lawsuit that homeowners insurance often excludes. See dirt bike insurance coverage.
  • A street-legal dual-sport must meet the state's motorcycle liability minimum, the same as any road bike.

Do You Need Insurance for a Dirt Bike? The 4-Location Test

The quickest way to answer 'do you need insurance for a dirt bike' is to ask where the tires will touch the ground:

1. Your own private land. In most states there's no legal requirement. Your homeowners policy may still exclude injuries involving motorized vehicles, so legal isn't the same as protected.
2. Someone else's private land. Some states, including Pennsylvania for registered ATVs, require liability insurance once you ride on property you don't own or lease. The landowner may also insist on it.
3. Public trails, state parks and OHV areas. This is the most regulated setting. States typically require registration or a trail permit, and several also require proof of liability insurance.
4. Public roads. A dirt bike on the road must be titled, registered and plated as street-legal, then insured like any motorcycle. Unplated dirt bikes generally can't be ridden on public roads at all.
Registration Isn't Insurance

An OHV sticker or trail permit (like Colorado's CPW registration) funds trail maintenance and identifies the machine. It doesn't pay for anyone's injuries or damage. Only a liability policy does.

What States Require Dirt Bike Insurance?

No official 50-state list exists, and the rules change, so treat this as a map of patterns and confirm with your state wildlife, natural-resources or motor vehicle agency:

States that require liability insurance in defined off-road situations:
• Pennsylvania: registered ATVs ridden on land not owned or leased by the rider (per PA DCNR). DCNR sets no minimum amount.
• New York: ATVs, which NY defines to include vehicles on two or more wheels, must be registered, and liability coverage is generally required when ridden off the owner's land. See the NY DMV ATV guide.
• Ohio: off-road vehicles must be registered with the BMV, and some public riding areas require proof of financial responsibility.
• Several other states (for example New Jersey and parts of the Mountain West) require insurance in state parks, on public lands, or when an OHV is registered for limited road use.

States where registration or permits apply but insurance generally doesn't (trail-only riding): Colorado, California and many others.

Street-legal (plated) dirt bikes: nearly every state requires motorcycle liability. Florida is a notable exception. See do you need insurance on a motorcycle in Florida. State minimums differ, as the Colorado and Ohio breakdowns show.
Parks and Private Tracks Can Be Stricter Than the State

Individual OHV parks, private motocross tracks and landowners often require proof of liability insurance, a signed waiver, or both, even in states with no mandate. Call ahead before you load the trailer.

Do Dirt Bikes Need Insurance If the Law Doesn't Require It?

Legally, no. Financially, it's often a good idea. The CPSC reports an annual average of more than 800 deaths and about 100,000 emergency-room-treated injuries involving off-highway vehicles. Three situations cause most of the financial damage:

• Lending the bike. A friend's kid gets hurt on your bike, and their parents' health insurer or lawyer comes after you through subrogation.
• Theft. Dirt bikes are light, rarely titled and easy to sell for parts. Only comprehensive pays. See does motorcycle insurance cover theft.
• Property damage. A runaway bike hits a neighbor's truck, fence or livestock.

Homeowners policies usually exclude motorized vehicles off the premises. A standalone off-road policy is typically cheaper than a street motorcycle policy. The details are in dirt bike insurance coverage, and four-wheel machines are covered in quad bike & ATV insurance.

Kids, Licenses and Dirt Bikes

Most states let minors ride dirt bikes on private land without a license, and many set age or engine-size limits for public OHV areas. Insurers can usually add young riders to an off-road policy, typically at a higher rate. For road-legal bikes, a license or permit is required, and it affects insurability. Read can you get motorcycle insurance without a license. If your family has several vehicles and policies, run them through the coverage gap checker to spot overlaps and holes.

Real-Life Case Incident & Precedent
Precedent: New York ATV registration and off-owner-property liability rules; homeowners motorized land vehicle exclusion

Illustrative Case: The Borrowed Pit Bike in Upstate New York

Scenario: The Harrisons let a neighbor's 15-year-old ride their son's registered 125cc dirt bike on an adjoining farm the family didn't own. He hit a fence post and broke his wrist and collarbone, with medical bills of $22,000. The Harrisons had no off-road policy, and their homeowners insurer cited the motorized-vehicle exclusion for off-premises use.

Resolution & Judicial Outcome: Because the bike was ridden off the Harrisons' own land, New York's ATV rules, which treat two-wheeled off-road machines as ATVs, generally required liability coverage they didn't have. The neighbor's health insurer paid the bills and then pursued the Harrisons for reimbursement. They settled for $9,500 out of pocket. An off-road liability policy would have cost a small fraction of that per year.

What You Should Do: Step-by-Step Action Plan

1 List every place you ride (your land, friends' land, public trails, tracks, roads) and check each against your state agency's rules.
2 Register the bike if your state requires it for public land, and carry proof on rides.
3 Ask your homeowners insurer in writing whether the dirt bike is covered on and off premises.
4 If not, buy an off-road policy with liability at minimum, plus comprehensive if the bike is valuable.
5 Never lend the bike to a rider your policy doesn't cover. Review the rules with the policy review checklist.

Critical Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing an OHV registration sticker with liability insurance.
  • Assuming homeowners insurance covers a dirt bike at the trailhead.
  • Riding an unplated dirt bike on public roads, which is illegal and leaves you uninsured in a crash. See what happens if you crash without insurance.
  • Relying on a list of 'states that require dirt bike insurance' without checking the specific park or track rules.

Can an insurance company drop you or cancel your policy after you file a claim?

In most jurisdictions, an insurer cannot immediately cancel your policy mid-term solely because you filed a single legitimate claim, unless there was proven fraud, material misrepresentation, or non-payment. However, they are legally permitted to non-renew your policy when the term expires (typically every 6 or 12 months) by providing advance written notice.

What happens legally and financially if you get into a car crash without insurance?

Driving without insurance and causing an accident results in catastrophic personal financial liability and severe legal penalties. You are personally liable for 100% of all medical bills, property damages, and legal judgments out of pocket. Legally, authorities can suspend your driver's license, impound your vehicle, impose thousands in fines, and mandate SR-22 high-risk insurance for years.
