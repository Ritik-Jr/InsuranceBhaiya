PEO Health Plan

A health plan offered through a Professional Employer Organization (PEO), which pools employees from many small businesses together under one master policy to access group rates closer to those of a large employer.

Actuarial & Contractual Mechanics

A PEO health plan works through what's called a co-employment arrangement: your business enters a contract with a PEO, which becomes the official 'employer of record' for HR, payroll, and benefits purposes, while your company keeps control of day-to-day work direction. Because the PEO combines employees from many client businesses into one large risk pool, even a five-person company can access benefits and pricing normally reserved for employers with hundreds of workers — a meaningfully different structure from a standard small-group policy purchased directly from a carrier, and from a level-funded plan, which stays entirely within one employer's own risk pool.

Real-World Dollar Scenario

A 12-person marketing agency can't get competitive group health rates on its own. It partners with a PEO, which combines the agency's employees with those of hundreds of other small client businesses into one large pool, letting the agency offer employees a large-employer-style plan (often including better provider networks and lower per-employee costs) that it couldn't have accessed independently.

Wallet Risk: What Happens If You Get This Wrong?

If you're a small-business owner comparing benefits options, a PEO plan can meaningfully close the pricing and plan-quality gap with larger employers, but it also means giving up some administrative control since the PEO becomes your employees' legal employer for benefits purposes — and if your business ever leaves the PEO arrangement, you'll typically need to set up a new standalone plan rather than simply keeping the same policy. A PEO health plan is one of the main ways a small business can access benefits and pricing that would otherwise only be realistic for a much larger employer.

The Co-Employment Structure, Explained

Entering a PEO arrangement means signing a co-employment contract: the PEO becomes the official 'employer of record' for payroll, tax filings, and benefits administration, while your business keeps full control over hiring, firing, and day-to-day work direction. Because the PEO combines employees from potentially hundreds of small client businesses into one large risk pool, even a very small company can access provider networks and per-employee pricing normally reserved for large employers.

How This Compares to Other Small-Business Options

A PEO plan is a structurally different path than a standard small-group policy or a level-funded health plan — those keep your business as the sole legal policyholder, while a PEO plan sits under one much larger master policy shared across many employers. See what does health insurance actually cover? and Marcus's small business health plan structure scenario for how a real small employer weighs this decision.

What Happens If You Leave the PEO

If your business ever exits a PEO arrangement, you typically can't simply keep the same policy — you'll need to set up a new standalone group plan, which is worth planning for well before any transition.

Regulatory & Editorial Notice: This entry is educational and general in nature. PEO contract terms, benefits, and pricing structures vary significantly by provider and should be reviewed with a licensed benefits advisor.
Quick Knowledge Check
What makes a PEO health plan different from a standard small-group health policy?
• PEO plans only cover Medicare-eligible employees
Incorrect. PEO plans are unrelated to Medicare eligibility and are used for working-age employees of small businesses.
The PEO pools employees from many small businesses into one large risk pool under co-employment
Correct. This pooling is what lets small employers access pricing and benefits closer to a large group plan.
• A PEO plan is always more expensive than a direct small-group policy
Not necessarily — PEO pooling often lowers per-employee cost precisely because the risk pool is larger, though results vary by PEO and industry.
• A PEO plan requires the business to become fully self-funded
Incorrect. The PEO, not the individual client business, holds the master policy and manages the funding structure.
