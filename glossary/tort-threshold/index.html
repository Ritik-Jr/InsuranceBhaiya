Tort Threshold

A tort threshold is the line you must cross in a no-fault state before you're allowed to sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering. It's either a 'verbal' definition of serious injury or a 'monetary' amount of medical bills.

Actuarial & Contractual Mechanics

No-fault insurance makes a trade: your own personal injury protection pays your bills quickly, and in exchange you give up the right to sue for minor injuries. The tort threshold is where that trade ends. Verbal thresholds list qualifying injuries. New York's 'serious injury' definition in Insurance Law §5102(d) includes a fracture, significant disfigurement, permanent limitation of a body function, and being unable to perform your usual daily activities for 90 of the first 180 days. Monetary thresholds open the courthouse door once your medical bills pass a dollar figure, for example $2,000 in Kansas and Massachusetts or $4,000 in Minnesota (ValuePenguin). Choice no-fault states (Kentucky, New Jersey and Pennsylvania) let you pay more to keep full tort rights, or less to accept a limited-tort threshold. At-fault states like Texas, Georgia and Colorado have no threshold at all. Compare the systems in no-fault vs at-fault car insurance states.

Real-World Dollar Scenario

Two New York drivers are rear-ended the same day. Derek has whiplash, needs 8 weeks of physical therapy and misses 3 weeks of work. His no-fault PIP pays his $6,500 in bills and his lost wages, but his injury doesn't meet the serious-injury threshold, so he can't sue for pain and suffering. Rita breaks her wrist. A fracture is a listed serious injury, so after PIP pays her bills she can also sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering. Her claim later settles for $60,000 on top of her no-fault benefits.

Wallet Risk: What Happens If You Get This Wrong?

If you misunderstand your threshold, you can make two costly mistakes. You might settle too early, before doctors confirm a qualifying injury like a herniated disc with significant limitation. Or you might pick 'limited tort' in a choice state just to save on premium without realising what rights you've given up. Always get a full medical workup and keep records that document limitation and missed days. The 90/180-day category in New York depends on proof. The claim steps are in how to file a no-fault insurance claim in New York.

Verbal vs Monetary Thresholds at a Glance

States with verbal thresholds include Florida, Michigan, New Jersey (limitation-on-lawsuit option), New York and Pennsylvania (limited tort). States with monetary thresholds include Hawaii, Kansas, Kentucky, Massachusetts, Minnesota, North Dakota and Utah. Monetary thresholds are easier to meet as medical costs rise, which is one reason some reform debates favour verbal thresholds. Quick Knowledge Check
In a no-fault state with a verbal tort threshold like New York, which injury would most likely let you sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering?
A fractured collarbone
Correct. A fracture is one of the listed 'serious injury' categories under NY Insurance Law §5102(d).
• Bruising that heals in two weeks
Minor soft-tissue injuries usually don't meet a verbal threshold. PIP covers the bills, but you can't sue for pain and suffering.
• Any injury, as long as the other driver was 100% at fault
That's the rule in at-fault states. In no-fault states, fault alone doesn't open the door to a lawsuit. The injury must meet the threshold.
• Damage to your car over $10,000
Vehicle damage claims are always fault-based and aren't limited by the tort threshold. The threshold applies to injury claims.
